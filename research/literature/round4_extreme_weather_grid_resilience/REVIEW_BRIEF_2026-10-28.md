# Round 4 research brief — climate extremes, electricity outages and restoration

**Created:** 28 October 2026. **Status:** independent literature-review candidate screening and scientific gap design; source pages/DOIs checked, **zero complete-paper critical appraisals**, no Kerala outage model and no peer-review submission.

## 1. Why this is the next different research gap

The first three literature themes ask whether electricity demand can be explained by weather, hydro operation has real water constraints, and renewable generation can be delivered through the grid. **Round 4 asks whether the grid remains operational during disruptive weather and how electrical service is restored when it fails.** It studies infrastructure and customer-service *consequences* of severe rain, floods, landslides, high winds and heat, with explicit factual protection against treating hypothetical resilience indices as real Kerala outcomes.

**Central research question:** *Can historically documented Kerala electricity-service losses, asset failures and restoration trajectories be reconciled into an auditable observed resilience evidence record, or are source and observational gaps large enough to prevent published engineering claims?*

**Potential deliverable (not a promised fourth manuscript):** a detailed source-quality and resilience-observability atlas, linked to event dates, original Kerala utility/State Planning Board reports, national infrastructure adaptation plans and methodological benchmarks. A narrower evidence atlas might be useful even if no measured restoration trajectory can be reconstructed.

## 2. Operational Kerala evidence and source differences

**Kerala State Planning Board / Mission Reconnect:** The *Economic Review 2019 Volume I* (R4-O01) reports that KSEBL repaired and reconnected extensive distribution infrastructure and consumers following the August 2018 floods and separately describes 2019 flood damage and restoration. The report references distribution transformers, damaged distribution lines and service connections; *specific counts/dates must be checked against original document pages before citation in a new scientific result*. An event-wide number of restored connections does **not** establish outage start times, individual interruption hours, common denominators, standardized SAIDI/SAIFI or actual energy not served.

**KSEBL annual administration report 2018–19 (R4-O07):** a useful independent source-family cross-check for Mission Reconnect and infrastructure repairs, subject to true original edition and indicator definitions.

**Kerala State Disaster Management Authority (R4-O04)** and **UN/ILO Kerala Post-disaster Needs Assessment 2019 (R4-O05)** contain events, damage and official recovery context but may use different financial, asset and service units. The presence of several official accounts does not make them definitionally interchangeable.

**India national planning:** The CEA *Disaster Management Plan 2025* (R4-O02), the *2024 Distribution Network Planning Criteria* (R4-O03), and the 18 August 2025 Ministry of Power parliamentary response (R4-O06) distinguish suggested/issued adaptation designs from demonstrated implementation. They do not certify actual Kerala network hardening or restore measured incident logs.

**Other Indian operator comparator:** Grid Controller of India's published storm Biparjoy 2023 operational case (R4-O08, published 2025) reports power-system operator preparation and restoration experience, but Gujarat/Indian regional records are not Kerala field data and the journal page does not display a DOI.

## 3. What the strongest papers already contribute (screening-level evidence only)

**India climate-risk assets — [Jindal, Kerkhofs and Shrimali (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2026.115697), R4-001.** Publisher reports asset-level climate physical risk analysis involving 424 generating assets and spatial flood protection counterfactuals, with Kerala among vulnerable coastal areas. It is a **modelled risk/financial-loss** analysis, not direct measurement of Kerala outage durations. This is direct prior art for climate vulnerability and should be reviewed first.

**Indian state resilience — [Odisha cyclone power-sector case (2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/cli8060073), R4-002.** Examines cyclone-driven damage to power infrastructure and governance using four resilience attributes. Distinct from algorithmic grid optimization. Strong historical counterpart but requires direct original event/source verification and no assumption that Odisha repair costs generalise to Kerala.

**India outage association — [Verschuur and Balakrishnan (2024)](https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2024-3176), R4-P01.** An **EGUsphere discussion preprint**, not verified as an accepted journal article. Authors describe four years of daily outages across 370 Indian locations and meteorological data. The highly relevant measurement source, outage definitions, regional coverage and possible final journal version must be investigated. Do **not** import headline percentages as Kerala effects.

**Extreme-event microgrid — [Nejati Amiri et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scs.2025.106290), R4-003.** Includes real input data and simulated cyclone stress with battery-life-aware two-stage microgrid operation. The modelled success is conditional on component availability and outages; it is not observed local restoration performance.

**Comparable primary-method precedent — [Zhu et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1287/ijds.2023.0017), R4-023.** Uses high-frequency actual customer power-outage data and corresponding weather over US service areas. Strong design comparator for what an instrumented field-evidence study requires, but transferred model coefficients would be scientifically invalid.

**Current outage measurements — [Scientific Reports (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-37040-w), R4-024.** Publisher describes 20 years and over 160,000 utility outage records, a weather-zone fragility and restoration-time study. The data source, missingness, hazard attribution and temporal generalization are the valuable methodology, not US location effect sizes.

**Fragility-method danger — [Dai and Preece (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.segan.2025.101845), R4-018.** Explicitly tests how health, asset interactions and evolving weather fields change modelled system resilience using a **synthetic 200-bus system**. This counsels against false precision from uncalibrated Kerala public-network skeletons.

**Definitions and metrics — [Paul et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rser.2023.113991), R4-008; [Umunnakwe et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rser.2021.111252), R4-009; [Liu et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44287-026-00298-3), R4-016.** These reviews cover resilience, reliability, restoration and actual sensor/outage evidence. They anchor terminology; they do **not** validate Kerala event reconstruction.

**Long-horizon climate adaptation — [Ruan et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44287-026-00322-6), R4-017.** Frames adaptation over multiple future climate realizations. Its long-horizon perspective cannot be used to claim a forecast of future Kerala outages without adequate locally calibrated hazard and asset failure functions.

## 4. Critical distinctions to preserve

| Quantity | What it actually means | Common unjustified substitution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hazard intensity | Flood depth, measured wind, extreme precipitation or temperature at a dated location | Treating nearby reanalysis exposure as failure cause for every line |
| Asset damage | Tower/line/transformer/substation physical condition at time of inspection | Calling all interrupted load physical equipment failure |
| Restoration count | Connections or assets recorded restored during response | Using totals as daily event duration or SAIDI |
| Outage minutes | Duration of interruption for a defined service/feeder and time window | Replacing minutes with one calendar-day power balance |
| Customer-hours interrupted | Sum of interruption durations weighted by affected connections | Using missing SLDC demand as equivalent customer-hours |
| Energy not served | Counterfactual load absent during interruption integrated over time | Treating annual sales gap as outage MWh |
| Simulated resilience index | Modelled performance under explicitly assumed hazard, fragility and repair functions | Publishing as observed grid reliability or certified N-1 |
| Adaptation benefit | Observed improvement or defensible matched counterfactual against a baseline | Claiming avoided outages solely from a proposed regulation |

*All variables require source custody, outage event clocks and unambiguous boundaries.*

## 6. Conditional novelty — not yet demonstrated

A defensible Kerala-specific contribution would require one of the following, each subject to primary data rights and method:
1. **Event-to-asset archive reconciliation** for 2018–19, comparing original source vintages, geographies, restored consumers, damaged assets and report revisions without conflating the reporting boundaries.
2. A **repeatable source quality method** that reveals how recorded outage/restoration quantities differ from modelled resilience metrics and what cannot be reconstructed from public sources.
3. A **calibrated power-service fragility/recovery study**, *only if* original incident time series, failure cause codes, sufficient climate data and network/asset dictionaries become available.

If only end-of-event totals exist, publish a transparent descriptive evidence atlas or technical report. **Do not** manufacture individual start/end outage timestamps, customer-hours lost, LOLE, feeder N-1, catastrophe costs or buildable infrastructure interventions.
