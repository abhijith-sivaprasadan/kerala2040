## 1. Research context: Kerala as an observability problem, not a model competition

Electricity-system planning in Kerala is simultaneously concerned with rapidly evolving loads, substantial reliance on electricity from outside the state, monsoon-linked hydropower and the changing need for flexible generation and interconnection. Institutional studies have already analyzed its pathways to a higher renewable-energy share (CSTEP, 2024), resource adequacy through the mid-2030s (CEA, official plan) and future clean-energy procurement (WRI India, 2026). These studies address an essential planning question, but their modeled future load chronology and capacity balance are not interchangeable with authenticated historical operational measurements. In particular, the relationship between statewide published daily energy statistics, daily national Power Supply Position records and actual hour-by-hour metered load must be resolved before calibrating claims about supply flexibility.

The empirical literature on Indian electricity and weather is more advanced than an initial Kerala-specific project might suppose. Harish, Singh and Tongia (2020) investigated temperature sensitivity using both national load-dispatch-centre data and disaggregated Delhi billing records. Their results demonstrate nonlinear and heterogeneous temperature relationships; importantly, reported load met may be constrained by rationed supply and should not automatically be treated as unconstrained demand. These results also illustrate why an ecological temperature–energy relationship cannot by itself identify household cooling access or an operational power-system shortage.

Hunt and Bloomfield (2025) provide an even closer antecedent. They developed machine-learning models of daily electricity demand for Indian states, including Kerala, using Grid-India reports, population-weighted reanalysis meteorology and explainable XGBoost modeling. Their open data and source code support scientific inspection, and the paper reports state-specific results and weather sensitivity. The existence of their Kerala analysis means that using ERA5, XGBoost and SHAP on Kerala daily electricity data is **not** in itself novel. Porwal and colleagues (2026) further combine Indian-state daily Power Supply Position reports with Open-Meteo historical weather, although a full-text methodological comparison remains outstanding. These preceding studies narrow the space for a useful new daily-demand weather paper.

## 2. A different question: what is being measured and which future-year errors are credible?

Published daily energy-demand performance numbers can be incommensurate even when expressed for the same geographic state. A report's 'energy met' field, a state load dispatch centre's daily operational 'consumption' field and annual consumer-side electricity sales may refer to different reporting boundaries, accounting periods or source-revision rules. Kerala2040's existing audit preserves the annual KSEBL-attributed consumer series separately from the daily Kerala SLDC operational record. These definitions must be formally crosswalked against *original records* before interpreting any discrepancy between series. The purpose is not to presume that one record is incorrect, but to identify precisely which comparisons are admissible.

The interpretation of predictive performance depends equally on the evaluation target. Hunt and Bloomfield's published generic code uses random training/test separation and transformations of reported daily demand to remove large socioeconomic variation. Such a design has legitimate uses when modeling conditional historical meteorological response; it is not identical to asking how a fixed mapping trained on earlier years performs on a later financial year in the original operational MU/day unit. Methodological studies by Cerqueira, Torgo and Mozetič (2020), Roberts and colleagues (2017), and Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo (2018) show why the split structure must follow the intended temporal dependence, stationarity and transfer task, without claiming that all cross-validation of dependent time series is universally invalid.

A prospective Kerala2040 study would therefore evaluate identical fixed calendar-only and calendar-plus-weather regressors on qualified daily observations in later fiscal-year windows, reporting annual errors and signed differences rather than selecting a single model's best pooled score. The historical model-development record matters: earlier Kerala2040 experiments inspected FY2024–25 daily weather and demand, and other earlier years may have affected exploratory research decisions. Consequently, a retrospective chronological holdout can be described as *out of sample relative to that fold's fitted parameters*, but must not be presented as an untouched prospective confirmation unless the entire feature-selection, data-processing and inference protocol was locked in advance.

## 3. Meteorological provenance and the limits of same-day explanatory information

A third issue is the physical integrity of weather covariates. ERA5 provides a modeled, observation-constrained reanalysis (Hersbach et al., 2020), not a direct set of local weather-station measurements or a weather forecast issued at a particular operational decision time. The original resolution, spatial selection and temporal definition of each feature affect interpretation. Cucchi et al. (2020) demonstrate the use of ERA5 in hourly bias-adjusted meteorological forcing, while ECMWF's provider guidance explicitly distinguishes instantaneous analyses, forecast accumulation and mean-rate fields. Hourly precipitation or shortwave accumulation cannot necessarily be assigned to a full midnight-to-midnight IST day by grouping timezone-shifted timestamps alone: the interval represented by the data and any boundary overlap must be independently verified.

Previous Indian-state work uses population-weighted weather. Kerala2040's proposed fixed equal-weight five-point series is a geographically transparent screening choice, not a demonstrated improvement in population or load representation. A scientifically meaningful sensitivity analysis, if original source data are available, would compare the conclusions under spatial weightings and retain even unfavourable outcomes. Without that validation, it would be inappropriate to interpret a weather variable's SHAP contribution as a measured population-average temperature elasticity, household cooling demand or an operational day-ahead forecasting benefit.

## 4. Proposed contribution — conditional, falsifiable and restricted

The proposed research question is **whether authenticated Kerala SLDC daily operating-consumption records provide a measurably different and more interpretable source-boundary and retrospective later-year weather-skill assessment than existing Indian-state modeling approaches**. That hypothesis has two linked components: (a) auditable mapping among original national and state reporting fields, including missingness and revision handling; and (b) a fixed matched-learner comparison of realized reanalysis weather against calendar baselines over predeclared fiscal-year tests. Because existing studies have already implemented similar features and algorithms, neither component should be called a new methodological contribution until original-source evidence shows a nontrivial change in a scientific conclusion.

The primary result would report signed per-year errors and uncertainty even if weather added no stable value. If published national and SLDC targets prove equivalent, or if the analysis reduces to a routine replay of previously demonstrated algorithms, this manuscript framing loses its claim to originality and must be withdrawn or changed. If the full five-point historical ERA5 input is not source-verified, the new multiyear empirical claim cannot proceed. The alternative is a source-quality study with independently demonstrable methods novelty; if even that is insufficient, the honest output is a source-audited technical report rather than an inflated peer-reviewed submission.
