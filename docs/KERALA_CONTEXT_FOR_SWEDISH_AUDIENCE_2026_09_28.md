# Kerala context for an international / Swedish audience

**Prepared:** 28 September 2026  
**Purpose:** give scale and place to the engineering results without turning Kerala into a tourism brochure or a Kerala-versus-India ranking.

## Meet Kerala

Kerala is a narrow tropical state on India's south-west coast, between the **Arabian Sea** and the **Western Ghats**. Malayalam is the principal language. Its geography moves over short distances from coastal cities, beaches and backwaters through densely settled lowlands and plantations to reservoirs, steep highlands and tropical forests.

The same landscape that gives Kerala its visual identity also shapes its electricity system:

- dense settlement makes land and transmission corridors scarce;
- the Western Ghats provide elevation, rainfall and river systems that support hydropower, while also containing ecologically sensitive terrain;
- the monsoon affects reservoirs, renewable resources, hazards and electricity demand;
- a long coastline and backwater system create both infrastructure opportunities and ecological constraints;
- electrification of transport, cooling and other services adds new electricity demand to a compact system.

Official context sources:

- Kerala Department of Economics & Statistics: [38,863 km² geographical area](https://ecostat.kerala.gov.in/page/key-statistics)
- Kerala Forest Department: [biodiversity and Western Ghats context](https://forest.kerala.gov.in/en/biodiversity/)
- Kerala Tourism: [geography, coast, rivers and backwaters](https://www.keralatourism.org/kerala-at-a-glance/)

"God's Own Country" may be mentioned as Kerala's established tourism slogan, but it is not used as a scientific description.

## Scale in Swedish perspective

These comparisons are included only to make unfamiliar numbers intuitive. Sweden and Kerala have very different climates, industrial structures, heating needs and electricity systems, so they are **not benchmarks for which system is better**.

| Indicator | Kerala | Sweden | Why it helps a Swedish reader |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Population | about **36 million** in the mid-2020s population projection | **10,613,531** in July 2026 | Kerala serves more than three times as many people |
| Geographical / land area | **38,863 km²** | a little over **400,000 km² of land** | Kerala concentrates its infrastructure into less than one-tenth the land area |
| Population density | roughly **930 people/km²** using the 2026 projection | about **26 people/km²** | land, corridors and siting are fundamentally different problems |
| Electricity consumption | **29.31 TWh**, FY2024–25 | **125.3 TWh**, 2024 excluding losses | Kerala uses far less electricity in total despite its larger population |
| Per-capita electricity consumption | **816 kWh/person/year**, FY2024–25 | roughly an order of magnitude higher | Kerala's stress is not explained by unusually high electricity use per person |

Sources:

- Kerala State Planning Board: [Economic Review 2025](https://spb.kerala.gov.in/en/7036/) and [Kerala Development Report 2026](https://spb.kerala.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/kerala-development-report_2026.pdf)
- Statistics Sweden: [population statistics](https://www.scb.se/BE0101-en) and [population density / county land area](https://www.scb.se/hitta-statistik/sverige-i-siffror/manniskorna-i-sverige/befolkningstathet-i-sverige/)
- Swedish Energy Agency: [final electricity statistics for 2024](https://www.energimyndigheten.se/nyhetsarkiv/2025/slutgiltig-statistik-for-el-och-fjarrvarme-2024/)

A useful scale calculation is:

> If average electricity use increased by only **100 kWh per person per year** across roughly 36 million people, annual demand would rise by about **3.6 TWh** — around one-eighth of today's Kerala electricity consumption.

This is an educational arithmetic illustration, not a forecast.

Another useful Swedish comparison: Jämtland County has about **49,205 km² of land** and around **133,000 residents**. Kerala is smaller in area but has hundreds of times as many residents. Stockholm County is much denser than most of Sweden, yet Kerala's state-wide population density is still more than twice Stockholm County's roughly 380 people/km². The point is spatial pressure, not social comparison.

## Culture and development: descriptive, not superlative

The public introduction should describe Kerala rather than rank it against the rest of India.

Useful context includes:

- Malayalam language and literature;
- long Indian Ocean trading connections;
- Hindu, Muslim, Christian and Jewish histories;
- Onam, boat races and local festival traditions;
- performing arts including Kathakali, Mohiniyattam and Theyyam;
- coconut, rice, seafood and spice-rich food cultures;
- a long history of public investment in health and education.

Kerala has historically recorded strong health, literacy and social-development indicators. These can be stated with sources where relevant, but the site should avoid "Kerala is better than India" framing and should not use "the Scandinavia of India" as a headline. If the historical Nordic comparison is mentioned at all, it should be a small contextual note rather than part of the project's identity.

## Landscape as energy-system context

The landscape chapter should connect beauty to engineering rather than use scenery as decoration.

### Western Ghats and forests

The Ghats are the origin of many river systems and provide the elevation and rainfall conditions behind Kerala's hydropower. They are also a biodiversity hotspot with forests, steep terrain and landslide exposure. This is why "build more hydro/transmission/wind in the mountains" cannot be treated as an unconstrained engineering option.

### Monsoon and water

The monsoon links electricity demand, reservoir conditions, solar output and hazards. This motivates treating weather as an input on **both demand and supply**, not as a decorative climate variable.

### Coast and backwaters

Kerala's coast and inland water system shape settlements, transport, fisheries and land use. They create opportunities for water transport and some forms of distributed infrastructure while adding wetland, coastal and community constraints that spatial models must respect.

### Heavy-mineral black sands

Parts of the southern coast around Chavara contain important heavy-mineral sand deposits. Kerala's Department of Mining and Geology lists **ilmenite, rutile, zircon, monazite and sillimanite** in the Chavara deposit and estimates about **0.82 million tonnes of monazite** within the major deposit.

Monazite can contain thorium and rare-earth elements. The public story should therefore say **"monazite-bearing heavy-mineral black sands"**, not imply that Kerala's beaches are uniformly "thorium beaches."

Source: Kerala Department of Mining & Geology, [Mineral Resources](https://dmg.kerala.gov.in/en/mineral-resources/).

This geology connects naturally to the project's KMML / circular-industry work.

## Kerala already contains pieces of the transition

These are **case studies and precedents**, not evidence for the cause of statewide load shedding.

### Cochin International Airport (CIAL)

CIAL reports **50 MW** of installed renewable-energy capacity. It exports excess daytime solar electricity to the grid, while a BESS project for nighttime use of solar energy is under development. That makes CIAL a compact example of a statewide question: abundant daytime solar does not remove the need to serve loads after sunset.

Sources: [CIAL 2025 AGM update](https://www.cial.aero/News-Updates/AGM2025), [CIAL 2024–25 annual report](https://cial.aero/ckeditor/upload/CIAL-Annual-Report-2024-25.pdf).

### Kochi Metro

Kochi Metro reports **5.389 MWp** of station/depot solar capacity. Its official project page describes the rooftop and ground-mounted systems and the financing arrangement.

Source: [Kochi Metro project page](https://corporate.kochimetro.org/the-project).

The research relevance is not that a metro causes present shortages. It is that transport decarbonisation moves more energy services onto the electricity system while distributed generation can offset part of that demand.

### Kochi Water Metro

Kochi Water Metro operates battery-electric water transport. Its FY2023–24 annual report says terminal roofs and pontoon canopies were designed for solar and **envisages** a total **17 MWp** PV deployment that could meet its energy needs.

The 17 MWp value is a stated future design concept, **not installed capacity**.

Source: [Kochi Water Metro FY2023–24 Annual Report](https://cdn-dev.watermetro.co.in/KMWL_Bilingual_Annual_Report_2023_24_bb6fbf9dce.pdf).

These examples belong in a "Kerala already contains pieces of the transition" section, not in the causal diagnosis.

## Capital scale: rupees made legible in Swedish money

A Swedish audience has little intuition for crore rupees. Public-facing project figures should therefore show:

1. the original official INR amount;
2. a dated approximate SEK equivalent;
3. the project stage — rough estimate, tender, sanction, support amount or completed cost;
4. the primary source.

Do **not** use the SEK conversion to imply that Kerala projects are universally cheap or well delivered. Historical delays and cost overruns exist. The defensible theme is **capital-constrained / budget-conscious infrastructure planning**, not "cheap but good."

For the current presentation, an illustrative **25 September 2026** conversion of roughly **₹1 crore ≈ SEK 1.03 million** is sufficient for order-of-magnitude context. The rate is an informational mid-market conversion, not a procurement rate. Source: [XE historical INR table, 25 September 2026](https://www.xe.com/en-ca/currencytables/?from=INR).

The useful engineering question is not "can Kerala build everything cheaply?" It is:

> **With limited capital, which interventions remove the most persistent system vulnerability per unit of money, land and environmental impact?**

That question links the budget context directly back to hydro scheduling, demand flexibility, storage, targeted network reinforcement and selectively placed new generation.
